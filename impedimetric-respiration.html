Measuring breathing through impedance

As the chest expands and contracts, its electrical impedance changes. Impedance describes how much the body opposes an alternating current. An impedimetric respiration system detects these changes electrically and converts them into a signal that follows the breathing pattern.

This laboratory project brought together a high-frequency sensing circuit and analogue signal processing. I worked on the circuit design in Altium, assembled the prototype on stripboard and tested the individual stages before checking the complete system.

Circuit stages

The schematic shows the signal path from left to right. The lab supplied the circuit schematic; my design work focused on its implementation and component layout before the physical build.

  • Current injection: apply a 32 kHz electrical signal through the electrode connections. Changes in impedance affect the measured voltage.
  • Signal reading: a differential amplifier measures the voltage difference and helps reject interference shared by both inputs.
  • Envelope detection: a Schottky diode and RC network extract the slow amplitude changes from the high-frequency signal.
  • Filtering and amplification: remove unwanted components and increase the breathing-related output to a measurable level.

From design to stripboard

I assembled and soldered the components on stripboard, connected the circuit stages and tested their behaviour with a signal generator and oscilloscope. The work included measuring filter responses, checking amplifier bias and saturation, and examining how the envelope detector responds to changes in frequency.

Integrating the stages showed why a circuit can behave differently from an ideal calculation. Component tolerances, loading between stages and wiring all affect the measured output. The differential amplifier also highlighted the importance of matching components to reject common interference.

Measured output

For the final test, I connected the complete circuit to a breathing simulator at the electrode inputs. The simulator was set to 30 breaths per minute, corresponding to one simulated breathing cycle every two seconds. The sensing input was a 32 kHz square wave.

The repeated rises and falls show the processed signal as the simulated impedance changes. The fast sensing carrier has been demodulated and filtered so that the output follows the slower simulated breathing pattern. This is a laboratory test using a simulator, rather than a recording from a person.

What I learned

Building the system made the link between circuit design and signal quality tangible. Amplifier gain and bias need to leave room for the signal without clipping. Component matching affects noise rejection, and the envelope-detector time constant must smooth the carrier while still following the slower changes.

My main layout improvements would be fewer jumper wires and copper-track cuts, more compact connections, and better-matched components. The project gave me hands-on experience with the analogue electronics needed to turn a small physical change into a usable measurement.

Project context

An individual educational hardware prototype completed in the Biomedical Instrumentation laboratory at Imperial College London. The schematic, test conditions and measurement procedure come from the laboratory handout; the completed-board photograph and measurement record come from my report, Realization of an Impedimetric Respiration System.

The project demonstrates circuit assembly and bench testing. It does not establish clinical accuracy or validation as a patient-monitoring device.
