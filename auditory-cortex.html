Comparative Evaluation of Spike-Prediction Methods in the Mouse Auditory Cortex

Comparing classical receptive-field models with stimulus-dependent neural encoding to predict responses to natural ultrasonic vocalisations.

How do auditory neurons encode natural sounds?

A receptive field describes which combinations of sound frequency and recent stimulus history influence a neuron’s response. Classical methods estimate a fixed filter or set of features. This project asks whether a receptive field that changes with the stimulus can better predict responses to natural mouse ultrasonic vocalisations.

My contribution was to model implementation and evaluation, including analysis of nonlinear response properties. This was an eight-author Imperial College London project supervised by Dr Andriy S. Kozlov. The study combines a predictive comparison with an analysis of what the models reveal about changing neuronal selectivity.

From vocalisations to neural responses

The dataset contains extracellular auditory-cortex recordings from anaesthetised C57BL/6J mice. Spike sorting and manual curation yielded 46 single units. The stimulus combined 519 natural ultrasonic syllables, separated by 100 ms silent gaps, and was presented at least 20 times per unit.

Sound waveforms were converted into time–frequency spectrograms. Spike times were binned and averaged across repeated presentations to form a peri-stimulus time histogram (PSTH): the response trace each model aimed to predict. The comparison evaluates held-out firing-rate predictions rather than exact timing of individual spikes.

Four ways to model the same encoding problem

MethodWhat it models
Spike-triggered average · STAA single filter estimated from the average stimulus preceding spikes.
Spike-triggered covariance · STCAdditional feature directions identified from changes in stimulus covariance.
Maximum noise entropy · MNEA static logistic model with linear and quadratic stimulus terms.
Dynamic STRF · dSTRFA convolutional firing-rate predictor whose local receptive field depends on the current stimulus.

STA and STC are computationally simple, but their usual recovery assumptions are challenged by the structured, non-Gaussian statistics of natural vocalisations. MNE relaxes the Gaussian-stimulus requirement while retaining a fixed set of features. The dSTRF framework allows the effective filter itself to change across stimulus contexts.

The STC implementation combined the STA projection with squared projections onto selected covariance eigenvectors using Ridge regression. MNE fitted a bias, linear filter and quadratic interaction matrix using negative log-likelihood, with five-fold jackknife fitting and early stopping.

Training and the matched comparison

For dSTRF, a separate causal 1-D convolutional network was trained for each neuron. Five hidden convolutional layers used 128 channels and ReLU activations, followed by a linear readout. Its 64-band mel-spectrogram covered 20–125 kHz. The network used 13 frames of history at 50 ms resolution, giving a 650 ms receptive field.

Training used Adam, mean squared error against the trial-averaged firing rate and leave-one-chunk-out validation. STA and STC used chronological 80/20 splits with a temporal gap; MNE reserved a random 20% of bins for testing. All methods were scored using Pearson correlation between predicted and observed held-out response traces.

Two comparisons separate model choice from temporal settings. In the original run, MNE used 15 ms bins and a 180 ms receptive field, while the other methods used coarser bins. In the matched run, all four used 50 ms bins, 13 lags and a 650 ms receptive field including the current frame.

“Matched” refers to these temporal settings. Frequency representations, response preparation, loss functions and validation procedures still differed between pipelines. The common settings were chosen around the dSTRF model and were not necessarily optimal for each alternative.

Looking inside the dynamic receptive field

After training, PyTorch automatic differentiation computed the Jacobian of predicted firing rate with respect to each spectrogram input. This yields a 13 × 64 local receptive field at each time point, showing how small changes in frequency and lag would affect the model’s prediction in that stimulus context.

  • Gain change: the same broad receptive-field pattern becomes stronger or weaker. This was quantified using variation in its overall magnitude.
  • Temporal hold: a pattern persists while shifting along the lag axis. Similarity before and after lag alignment tests for this form of temporal persistence.
  • Shape change: tuning changes that remain after accounting for gain and lag shifts. An SVD-based complexity measure summarises the remaining diversity.

Under matched temporal settings, dSTRF exceeded STA in 34 of 46 neurons, STC in 31 of 46 and MNE in 35 of 46. Paired Wilcoxon tests favoured dSTRF against all three static approaches after Holm correction (adjusted p < 0.01).

The dynamic-field analysis found temporal hold in 20 of 46 neurons (43.5%), with observed persistence up to 150 ms. Gain varied across stimulus contexts, and all neurons showed substantial receptive-field shape diversity beyond simple gain and lag changes. The dynamic model therefore provided a way to describe changing tuning as well as predict responses.

Why temporal resolution changes the conclusion

MNE’s original 15 ms configuration achieved median r = 0.335, close to dSTRF’s 0.339. Their paired comparison was not significant (p = 0.1248). Coarsening MNE to the matched 50 ms configuration reduced its median score to 0.290; the original configuration performed better in 36 of 46 neurons.

This is a conditional advantage for dSTRF under the tested settings. Temporal resolution, response representation and loss function contributed to the gap. In sparse firing-rate data, the balance between silent and active bins also affects training. The findings do not isolate model architecture as the sole cause of improved prediction.

The recordings come from anaesthetised mice responding to a particular natural-vocalisation stimulus set. Broader generalisation would require additional datasets and more closely aligned evaluation pipelines. An interpretation I took from the study is to assess prediction accuracy and the biological behaviour exposed by a model together.
