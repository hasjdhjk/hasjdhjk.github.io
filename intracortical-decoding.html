Keeping a decoder useful across sessions

Brain–machine interfaces need a stable mapping from neural activity to intended movement. A model that predicts movement well on its training day may deteriorate as the recorded signals and their relationship to movement change. This independent project investigates how neural feature choice, decoder architecture and a small amount of new calibration data affect that transfer.

I developed an offline analysis across 14 experiment notebooks: inspecting recordings, establishing a temporal Ridge baseline, measuring cross-session drift, testing calibration strategies and comparing a linear decoder with a recurrent neural network.

Recordings, targets and temporal history

The analysis uses public LINK motor-cortex recordings in NWB format through the DANDI Archive. Eight centre-out sessions from one macaque span approximately three years. One original session provides training data; seven later sessions test transfer. The two outputs are index-finger velocity and the combined middle/ring/small-finger (MRS) velocity.

Neural inputs are sampled at 50 Hz. Each prediction uses 10 consecutive samples, giving 200 ms of history from 96 channels. Ridge receives 960 flattened inputs; the GRU receives the same information as an ordered 10 × 96 sequence. Threshold crossings and spiking-band power are compared as two alternative neural representations.

From a fixed decoder to limited-data adaptation

The experiments separate three sources of improvement: adding temporal information, correcting input distributions and learning a labelled movement correction. A fixed decoder and its original normalisation are first applied to later sessions without updates.

  • Temporal baseline: a validation search selected neural history, lag and Ridge regularisation. Adding 200 ms of threshold-crossing history raised same-session test R² from approximately 0.118 to 0.268; the selected model used no additional movement lag.
  • Normalisation only: estimate a new input mean and scale from unlabelled neural data in a later session, while keeping decoder coefficients fixed. This tests how much transfer failure comes from changes in input distribution.
  • Labelled residual correction: retain the original decoder and fit a Ridge model to its prediction errors using a short calibration segment. The adapted prediction adds this learned correction to the frozen decoder output.

Calibration budgets are 10, 30, 60 and 180 seconds. Five windows at each duration are distributed within the first half of each later session, while the final 40% provides the evaluation period. This gives 140 calibration windows across seven later sessions. Correction strength is chosen using chronological validation inside the calibration window.

The original session is divided chronologically into 70% training, 15% validation and 15% testing. The feature experiment refits Ridge on the first 85% after selecting regularisation; the Ridge–GRU comparison retains the first 70% for fitting. These two experiment families are reported separately.

Neural features: stability and adaptability

Threshold crossings and spiking-band power achieved similar performance within the original session: mean test R² was 0.268 and 0.272, respectively. Their behaviour changed when the same models were transferred to later recordings.

Spiking-band power transferred less reliably with a completely fixed decoder, but normalisation recovered much of that loss. With tuned residual adaptation, it outperformed threshold crossings in all seven later sessions at every calibration duration. Better fixed transfer and better adaptability were therefore different properties in this experiment.

The exact paired tests gave unadjusted p = 0.0156 and Holm-adjusted p = 0.0625 across the four calibration budgets. The observed direction was consistent, but it did not reach the corrected 0.05 significance threshold.

Ridge versus a temporal GRU

Both architectures use spiking-band power and identical 200 ms input windows. Ridge is a regularised linear mapping. The GRU processes the sequence with 32 hidden units and a linear readout for the two velocities; its hidden state starts fresh for each window. Validation performance selects the training epoch, with early stopping after 10 epochs without improvement.

GRU training was repeated across five random seeds to check sensitivity to initialisation and training-batch order. Every seed exceeded the Ridge reference on the original session’s held-out test period.

The GRU’s within-session advantage did not produce stronger median performance after cross-session residual adaptation. At 30, 60 and 180 seconds, median paired differences favoured Ridge for every GRU seed. This comparison concerns a linear correction applied to frozen models; it does not evaluate GRU fine-tuning.

Reading the results

R² measures how well predicted velocities explain variation in the recorded movement. A negative value means the predictions perform worse than a constant test-set mean. Scores summarise both velocity targets; the reported cross-session medians first reduce the five calibration-window repeats within each session.

The GRU within-session value is mean ± standard deviation over five training seeds. Its adaptation range spans five seed-specific session medians. The feature-comparison shading instead shows the interquartile range across sessions. These summaries describe different sources of variation and should not be treated as confidence intervals or pooled into a single model ranking.

Scope and current review

The central finding is that stronger within-session accuracy did not guarantee stronger performance after transfer and adaptation. Neural representation, distribution shifts and calibration budget all affected the outcome.

The experiment series and saved summaries are complete. These remain descriptive offline results from one macaque and seven later test sessions. They do not establish closed-loop BMI performance or a general advantage of linear over nonlinear decoding.

Timestamp alignment, overlapping history at split boundaries and preprocessing within calibration validation remain under methodological review. The figures present the saved results before that review is completed. Training seeds and repeated calibration windows do not increase the number of animals or recording sessions.
